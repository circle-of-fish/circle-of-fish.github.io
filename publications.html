Interpolity Orders and Their Organizing Principles

Reconstructs orders that were not systems of sovereign states — the Ming–Chosŏn ritual order, Warring States tianxia, the inter-imperial Europe from which Franco's Spain had been disengaged — and asks what actually held each together: virtue, status, ritual reciprocity, imperial identity. The cluster is where the collective builds general theory (aretocracy, the virtue of state, status ascent, symbiotic statehood) out of non-European historical material rather than measuring that material against Westphalia.

  • Inho Choi. 2025. Non-Western Interpolity Orders and Sociocultural Forces: the shi in the Early Modern East Asian Order. The Chinese Journal of International Politics 18(4): 496-519.
    Argues against the state-centric premise that empires and states alone make international order: transnational sociocultural forces did so too. From the Song period the literati (shi) of China, Korea and Vietnam became a transnational class increasingly autonomous from the state, producing what Choi calls a 'symbiotic form of state'; the shi then shaped the East Asian order both by steering the foreign-policy orientations of those symbiotic states and by underwriting the normative foundations of tributary relations. He concludes that a historical-sociological attention to sociocultural forces uncovers dynamics in non-Western interpolity orders that state-centric models cannot see.

    Argues that status ascent cannot be explained by a state's strategy alone: whether a status-seeker rises or falls depends on the synchronization between its strategy and the international social structure surrounding it during a period of order transition.   • Inho Choi (최인호). 2023. 사대의 국제정치이론: 일어난 미래로서 병자호란 [International Theory of Serving the Great: 1636 Qing Invasion of Chosŏn as a Future-Passed]. 국제정치논총 [Korean Journal of International Relations] 63(1): 43-86.
    Argues that sadae (serving the great), the principle governing Ming–Chosŏn relations until the 1636 Qing invasion, contains the seed of a post-sovereign international theory that historians and IR scholars have missed by reading it only within its own context as a specimen of the 'tributary system.' The reconstruction is offered as an alternative field of IR knowledge production and a contribution to the discipline's pluralization.

  • Inho Choi. 2022. 'Chinese' hegemony from a Korean shi perspective: aretocracy in the early modern East Asia. International Relations of the Asia-Pacific 22(3): 347-374.
    Argues that pre-modern 'Chinese' hegemony was not the unilateral normative projection of the Chinese state but a reciprocal rule of virtue — aretocracy — driven by the transnational sociocultural elite, the shi. Chinese and non-Chinese shi co-produced the hegemonic order out of a shared civilizational heritage, and within it authority was legitimate only for those who demonstrated excellence in civil virtues; the Ming and Chosŏn shi built a tradition of envoy-poetry exchange as the medium of that co-construction. Choi draws the normative implication that world-order making can rest on cultivated ethos rather than on state power alone.

  • Inho Choi. 2022. Virtue Rules: The Evolution of the Virtue of State in East Asian International Orders. Johns Hopkins University (Ph.D. dissertation, Department of Political Science) 390 pp..
    Argues that the 'virtue of state' is simultaneously a crucial form of state power and a constituent of international order: it confers power by making a state the leader in cultivating collective attunement among states, and it orders them by finely attuning them to social and natural forces — which especially advantages weak states in a hegemonic order, since virtue can be cultivated without material resources. States cultivate virtue through a dialectic of performance and evaluation within an 'evaluative community,' which requires the state to possess a fictional moral personhood. Choi traces three such fictions — the monadic, the sovereign and the liberal state — showing that the moral fiction of state arose separately in Warring States China and early modern Europe before the two traditions merged in nineteenth-century East Asia, where scholars translated legal sovereignty into a state virtue to cope with imperialism, and that liberal internationalism functions as a further state fiction through which the South Korean public and liberal networks cultivate liberal virtue. The payoff is an alternative conception of state agency: a flexible repertoire of virtues in place of the ineffectual pursuit of sovereignty.

Ontological Complexity and the Encounter with Others

Pushes past multiculturalism — the assumption that one reality is merely represented in many idioms — to argue that participants in a single order can inhabit genuinely different lived worlds, as the Chosŏn envoys and the Panchen Lama did at Qianlong's birthday. The same move is then turned on the present: on how a researcher must be undone by an infinitely distant Other, on U.S.–China rivalry as an encounter of ontological others, and on a divided peninsula read as a pre-individual field rather than two finished states.

  • Inho Choi and Minju Kwon. 2025. Ontological Complexity of Interpolity Orders: The Encounter between Chosŏn and Tibet in Qing. European Journal of International Relations 31(1): 28-52.
    Reframes the Qing order as ontologically complex rather than merely culturally diverse: existing multiculturalist accounts assume one imperial reality represented in many idioms, whereas the authors argue that peripheral participants inhabited genuinely different lived worlds. Reading Pak Chiwŏn's travelogue against Tibetan records of the Chosŏn envoys' meeting with the Panchen Lama at Qianlong's birthday ceremony, they show the Confucian envoys experienced the Lama as subhuman while the Lama experienced them as ignorant lay beings; observing this dissonance, Pak criticized the Qing court's appropriation of peripheral ontologies and proposed inhabiting other ontologies to foreground the pluriverse. The article argues an ontological approach can reconceptualize interpolity order generally, beyond the Qing case.

Race, Gender, and the Standards of Civilization

The collective's second research module, taken at the level where hierarchy is actually lived: bobbed hair in colonial Korean magazines, Japan's trade of naval tonnage for civilizational standing after the racial equality proposal failed, a Geneva Protocol article that spares mothers the noose but not the sentence. Read together the papers argue that civilizational and racial standards were co-produced by the people they ranked — colonized women, non-Western elites, fans and survivors — and that agency in these settings takes forms that neither resistance nor complicity describes.

  • Minju Kwon. 2026. All but the Death Penalty: Incomplete Protection for Civilian Internee Mothers. Feminist Legal Studies Online First (published 26 May 2026).
    Argues that unrecognized gender bias is built into the codification of international humanitarian law itself. Focusing on Article 76(3) of the 1977 Additional Protocol I, which forbids executing pregnant women and mothers of dependent children who are interned but still permits the death sentence to be pronounced against them, Kwon draws on the theory of benevolent sexism to contend that this 'preferential protection' encodes an essentialist picture of women as weak protectees by default. She further argues such benevolent protection predictably lapses once it collides with state interests in retaining discretion over women whose conduct exceeds traditional gender roles.

  • Minju Kwon. 2025. Short-Haired Modern Girls: Colonial Korean Women's Fashion as the Standards of Civilization. European Journal of International Relations 31(3): 561-585.
    Argues that gendered actors -- above all colonized women -- actively constituted the interwar standard of civilization rather than merely being measured against it, and that everyday politics of fashion was a site where that standard was contested. Reading debates over women's short hair in colonial Korean magazines and newspapers, Kwon shows that the so-called Modern Girls interpreted and practiced civilizational standards under three stacked hierarchies at once (non-Western polity, colonized nation, patriarchal society), defending bobbed hair as economically efficient, hygienic, aesthetically valuable, and emancipatory while being ceaselessly degraded for failing to embody 'genuine' modernity. The article thereby recasts the plurality of modernity through women's fashioned bodies and pushes Global IR to credit agency in non-Western, non-imperial societies.

  • Iara Gonzalez-Ascencio and Minju Kwon. 2025. Cohabitation with Criminals: Civilian Women's Everyday Cooperation with Mexican Drug Cartels. International Feminist Journal of Politics 27(2): 304-326.
    Argues that scholarship on women under narco-violence has been trapped between two extremes -- active resistance or direct cartel participation -- while most civilian women in fact act somewhere in between. Drawing on 37 semi-structured interviews in Jalisco, the authors develop the concept of 'everyday cooperation' and sort women's cohabitation with cartels into three modes: keeping silent, pursuing benefits, and idealizing narco-culture.   • Inhwan Oh. 2025. Japan's Status-Driven Restraint Despite Racial Humiliation, 1921–1936. The Journal of American-East Asian Relations (Brill) 32(3): 239-267.
    Against status-and-race theories that predict racial humiliation will push a rising power toward revisionism, Oh asks why Japan accepted an inferior naval ratio and stayed committed to the Washington naval system from 1921 to 1930 despite the 1919 rejection of its racial equality proposal and the 1924 Japanese Exclusion Act. He argues, in a constructivist-realist register, that Japan's orthodox foreign policy idea prioritized its higher *civilizational* status relative to both the Western powers and the rest of East Asia, producing an elite consensus among civilian and military leaders that accepted a trade-off of reduced military capability for increased status. Restraint therefore persisted until that elite consensus collapsed and the political-economic shock of the Showa Depression defeated social expectations in the early 1930s — showing that racial humiliation alone does not time revisionism.

  • Minju Kwon and Kaye Valdez. 2024. Sarcasm or Sexism? Media Framing of Duterte's Misogynistic Speeches. Asian Journal of Women's Studies 30(2): 85-109.
    Shows that Philippine news media systematically defused Rodrigo Duterte's misogynistic public speech rather than reporting it as abuse.   • Minju Kwon and Ya Su. 2024. Relatively Unworthy Victims? Middle-Aged Women as Rape Survivors. Violence Against Women 30(8): 1804-1824.
    Demonstrates through an online survey experiment in China that the age of a rape survivor changes how seriously the public treats the crime: respondents judged rape as less serious, and requested shorter sentences, when the survivor was a middle-aged woman than when she was younger, older, or of unstated age. International Law, Institutions, and Contested Legitimacy

Follows legitimacy as something armed groups, states and humanitarian gatekeepers must continually manufacture before domestic and international audiences — rebels signing UN action plans on child soldiers, a vigilante militia in Borno, the ICRC deciding whether to break its own confidentiality, five Northeast Asian claimants using the UN Charter against one another. It supplies the collective's institutional and legal register, showing that formal international machinery runs on recognition contests rather than on compliance alone.

  • Jeeye Song. 2023. Treaty-Making and Colonization in East Asia: Vietnam and Korea in the 19th Century. Ph.D. dissertation, Department of Political Science, University of Florida.

  • Minju Kwon. 2019. Blacklisted Rebels: Commitment to Child Rights in Armed Conflict. Ph.D. dissertation, Department of Political Science, University of Notre Dame (committee: Gary Goertz, Tanisha M. Fazal, Emilia Justyna Powell).

Emotion, Trauma, and Memory

Treats affect as a political practice with a history of its own — anger organized in the streets of 1960 Tokyo, deference performed across a negotiating table, the time structure of traumatic memory in testimony about wartime sexual violence — and asks what emotion studies becomes once non-Western voices are inside it rather than cited by it.
