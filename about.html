About the Circle

A friendly rejoinder to Kautilya

But when the law of punishment is kept in abeyance, it gives rise to such disorder as is implied in the proverb of fishes (matsyanyayamudbhavayati); for in the absence of a magistrate (dandadharabhave), the strong will swallow the weak; but under his protection, the weak resist the strong. Kauṭilya, Arthaśāstra, Book I, ch. 4, tr. R. Shamasastry (1915)

The circle of the fish is our friendly rejoinder to Kautilya's law of fish. In a South Asian rendering of the law of the jungle in anarchy, Kautilya, in the Arthashastra, says that life without a state is like the life of fish living in a pond filled with predatory drives. The law of the fish prevails when big ones swallow small ones.

In the Hobbesian anarchy, states, too, live under the law of fish. Unfortunately, however, Kautilya did not bother to ask the fishes themselves what it is really like to live as a fish, just as Hobbes did not ask wolves. A gang of puffers would not have recognized themselves in Kautilya's law of fish. They live rather constructive and artistic lives, building circles with elaborate patterns — the ones you see on this page.

We are junior South Korean IR scholars who are like these puffers. Many of us do not recognize ourselves in the theories and narratives of the current IR discipline. While we study disparate subjects, this ontological concern unites us.

At the same time, we are also not puffers. We do not know what it really is like to live as puffers. Thus, we want to learn their way and the way of many others beyond puffers: Chinese, Japanese, Europeans, Americans, indigenous peoples, non-humans, cyborgs, oceans, shells, aliens.... This learning is also our politics. Inter-world politics along with international politics.

But we do begin from East Asia, as it is the closest. In short, we aim to globalize IR by exploring ontologies in East Asia and beyond.

Aims

What the Circle works toward

  • 01Recover marginalized ontologies in East Asian international relations
  • 02Connect indigenous East Asian discourses on the international with current IR theories
  • 03Bring long-term historical East Asian international relations into the history of the modern international order
  • 04Contribute to the pluralization of IR by way of studying East Asian international relations

How We Work

Reading, drafting, and arguing together since 2020

The Circle met for the first time in June 2020 and has read together roughly once a month ever since. A session usually takes one book or a cluster of articles and works through it in a single long sitting; the reading list on the Resources page is simply the residue of five years of those sessions.

The other half of the calendar belongs to our own work. Members bring drafts — dissertation chapters, article manuscripts, project proposals — and the group reads them the way a hostile reviewer would, before the journal does. Several of the articles listed under Publications passed through this room first.

Periodically we convene something larger: a summer seminar, a methodology workshop on reading East Asian diplomatic documents, a pedagogy workshop on teaching IR and Asian Studies together. Members are spread across South Korea and North America, so we meet online as a matter of course and in person when we can.
