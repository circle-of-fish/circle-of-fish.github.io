Research

Orientation, method, and two research modules

What holds the Circle together is not a shared object of study but a shared suspicion: that the entities the discipline takes for granted — sovereign states in anarchy, pursuing security under a single logic of survival — are one historically particular way of carving up the international, mistaken for the only one. Everything below follows from taking that suspicion seriously.

Theoretical Orientation

Four commitments

These are not doctrines the Circle enforces. They are the recurring moves our published work turns out to make, stated plainly enough that a reader can hold us to them.

01

Ontology comes before theory

Before asking how units behave, we ask what counted as a unit at all, and what kinds of relation were thinkable between them. An order in which a polity could be simultaneously a kingdom, a vassal, a ritual junior, and a trading counterpart is not a defective states-system; it is a differently constituted one. Reading such orders on their own ontological terms is the Circle's first move, not a preliminary to the real analysis.

02

Indigenous concepts are theory, not local colour

Sadae (事大, serving the great), shi (勢, propensity of things), li (禮, ritual propriety), tianxia (天下, all-under-heaven): we treat these as working analytical vocabularies that generated their own accounts of hierarchy, obligation, and change — not as folk beliefs awaiting translation into realism or constructivism. The test of such a concept is the same as for any other: what can it explain that the alternatives cannot?

03

Global IR means complexifying theory, not adding cases

Appending an East Asian chapter to an otherwise unchanged theory leaves the theory unchanged. We are after the harder outcome: concepts built from East Asian material that travel back and unsettle the general categories — hegemony, sovereignty, hierarchy, order — rather than sitting quietly beside them as regional exceptions.

04

Hierarchy is constitutive, not deviant

Race, gender, status, and the standards of civilization are not frictions in an otherwise egalitarian order of states; they are among the materials the order was built from. Following that through means asking how racial humiliation shaped a rising power's restraint, how a colonial woman's haircut became a measure of civilizational standing, and how such measures were written into international institutions and law.

Methodological Orientation

How we actually work

An ontological argument is only as good as the reading that supports it. The Circle's methodological commitments are therefore mostly commitments about sources: which ones, in which language, read how.

01

Multilingual archives, read against each other

The record of East Asian interpolity relations is documentary and irreducibly multilingual: Literary Chinese court histories and statutes, Korean veritable records and envoy diaries, Japanese and Manchu materials, Iberian and Anglophone consular correspondence. We work with these in the original and, wherever possible, read one archive against another, since the same event is rarely the same event twice.

02

以意逆志 — reading toward the writer's intent

The Circle devoted a 2023 methodology workshop to iuiyeokji, the Mencian precept that a reader should not let the wording defeat the sentence, nor the sentence defeat the intent, but should meet the writer's intent with one's own understanding. Taken as a method for diplomatic history, it disciplines a specific temptation: reading a memorial or an envoy's report as though its formulaic surface were either transparent evidence or empty ritual, when the work is to reconstruct what a writer was trying to do with those formulas.

03

Conceptual history and the politics of translation

Sovereignty, independence, equality, civilization: each arrived in East Asia as a translation, and each translation was a settlement of a dispute. Following Koselleck's layered time and Lydia Liu's account of translingual encounter, we track how such terms were coined, contested, and repurposed — and treat the moment of translation as the point where an ontological conflict becomes visible in the record.

04

The seminar as method

Almost everything the Circle has published was first read aloud to the Circle in draft and taken apart. That is not collegiality but method: an argument built from an unfamiliar archive needs readers who will ask whether the source really says that, and readers who know the neighbouring archive well enough to check. The seminar list on this site is, in that sense, the apparatus behind the publication list.

Research Modules

Two collaborative programmes

Module 1

Power, Institution, and Ideas in Historical East Asian International Orders

This module pursues a systematic theorization of material, ideational, and institutional structures and their changes in historical East Asian international orders, from the early modern period to the early twentieth century. Compared with the established studies of early modern and modern Europe, work on material, ideational, and spatial structures in East Asia remains rudimentary and undertheorized.

The existing literature has focused largely on comparing distinctive East Asian organizing principles with the European sovereign states system and the balance of power. Drawing on the burgeoning historical scholarship on East Asian international orders, this module instead investigates the operating components of those structures and their continuity and transformation at critical historical junctures.

Research areas
  • Intellectual and conceptual history of East Asian international law and thought
  • Interconnected maritime powers and maritime order in East Asia and beyond
  • Power, diffusion, and topos in East Asia
  • Status and institutional hierarchies in East Asian international orders
Module 2

Race and Gender in Historical East Asian International Politics

This module explores racial and gendered inequalities in historical East Asian international politics — not as a supplement to the study of power and institutions but as an account of how that power was constituted and that hierarchy made legible.

Research areas
  • Decolonial and pluriversal approaches to race and gender
  • Reassessing racialized and gendered violence under Japanese imperialism and wartime occupation
  • The role of gender norms and of women in the making of international society
  • How Japanese colonial rule imposed racial hierarchies in East Asia in the context of Western racial ideologies
  • Eurocentric racial biases in international institutions and international law
