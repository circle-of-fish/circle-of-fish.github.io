Circle of the Fish

Globalizing IR from East Asia

Kautilya never asked the fish what it is like to live as one.

We are junior South Korean scholars of International Relations. We work to globalize IR by recovering the ontologies through which East Asia — and the many worlds beyond it — have understood the international.

The Law of the Fish, Reconsidered

Why we are called the Circle of the Fish

The circle of the fish is our friendly rejoinder to Kautilya's law of fish. In a South Asian rendering of the law of the jungle in anarchy, Kautilya, in the Arthashastra, says that life without a state is like the life of fish living in a pond filled with predatory drives. The law of the fish prevails when big ones swallow small ones. Kautilya, Arthashastra — the matsya nyāya, or law of the fish

In the Hobbesian anarchy, states, too, live under the law of fish. Unfortunately, however, Kautilya did not bother to ask the fishes themselves what it is really like to live as a fish, just as Hobbes did not ask wolves. A gang of puffers would not have recognized themselves in Kautilya's law of fish. They live rather constructive and artistic lives, building circles with elaborate patterns — the ones you see on this page.

We are junior South Korean IR scholars who are like these puffers. Many of us do not recognize ourselves in the theories and narratives of the current IR discipline. While we study disparate subjects, this ontological concern unites us.

At the same time, we are also not puffers. We do not know what it really is like to live as puffers. Thus, we want to learn their way and the way of many others beyond puffers: Chinese, Japanese, Europeans, Americans, indigenous peoples, non-humans, cyborgs, oceans, shells, aliens.... This learning is also our politics. Inter-world politics along with international politics.

But we do begin from East Asia, as it is the closest. In short, we aim to globalize IR by exploring ontologies in East Asia and beyond.

Where We Stand

Theoretical and methodological commitments

What holds the Circle together is not a shared object of study but a shared suspicion: that the entities the discipline takes for granted — sovereign states in anarchy, pursuing security under a single logic of survival — are one historically particular way of carving up the international, mistaken for the only one. Everything below follows from taking that suspicion seriously.

Theoretical
  1. Orders are ontologically complex, not merely culturally diverse
  2. Interpolity order, not the international system
  3. Order is made by sociocultural forces, not by states alone
  4. Hierarchy is racialized, gendered, and worn on the body
  5. Pluralization by complexification, not by national schools
Methodological
  1. 以意逆志 (iuiyeokji): reading intention against intention
  2. Multilingual archival work across incommensurable record sets
  3. Conceptual history and the future-passed
  4. Historical process tracing and comparative case work
  5. Interpretive social science and knowing-from-within

  • Inhwan Oh and Inho Choi. forthcoming. Historical Intersections of Climatic Change and Power Transitions: Ecological Pressure, Balance of Adaptability, and the U.S.-China Power Transition in the Anthropocene. International Relations of the Asia-Pacific (Oxford University Press).
    Co-authored with Inho Choi, this article brings climatic change into power-transition theory by arguing that ecological pressure is a recurring driver of transition outcomes and that what determines who prevails is not raw material capability but a 'balance of adaptability' — the relative capacity of rival orders to absorb and reorganize under environmental stress. The authors read historical cases of climate-stressed transitions forward to the contemporary U.S.-China case in the Anthropocene. Listed as forthcoming on Oh's July 2026 CV; his March 2026 CV recorded it at Revise & Resubmit, so the acceptance is recent and no volume/DOI has been assigned yet.
  • Minju Kwon. 2026. All but the Death Penalty: Incomplete Protection for Civilian Internee Mothers. Feminist Legal Studies Online First (published 26 May 2026).
    Argues that unrecognized gender bias is built into the codification of international humanitarian law itself. Focusing on Article 76(3) of the 1977 Additional Protocol I, which forbids executing pregnant women and mothers of dependent children who are interned but still permits the death sentence to be pronounced against them, Kwon draws on the theory of benevolent sexism to contend that this 'preferential protection' encodes an essentialist picture of women as weak protectees by default. She further argues such benevolent protection predictably lapses once it collides with state interests in retaining discretion over women whose conduct exceeds traditional gender roles.
